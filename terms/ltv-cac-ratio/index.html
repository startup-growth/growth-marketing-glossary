Economics and monetization

LTV:CAC Ratio

LTV:CAC compares the estimated economic value of a customer with what it costs to acquire that customer. It can help decide whether growing a particular customer segment is sustainable, but the ratio needs an honest LTV calculation and a complete CAC definition.

Two businesses with the same ratio

Company A spends $400 to acquire a customer and expects $1,200 in lifetime gross profit. Its LTV:CAC is 3:1.

Company B also reports 3:1, but collects almost all of the gross profit over three years, while Company A earns most of it within six months. If both companies have limited cash, the second business faces a very different funding problem.

One way to examine that problem is CAC payback. If a customer contributes $40 per month after the relevant service costs, recovering a $400 CAC takes 10 months, assuming the contribution continues.

Don't treat 3:1 as a magic threshold

A frequently repeated SaaS rule of thumb says 3:1 is healthy. It is not a universal requirement. The right ratio depends on margins, growth ambitions, cash, product risk and how reliably the lifetime value estimate predicts the future.

Check whether LTV is revenue or gross profit. Make sure CAC includes the intended expenses. Most importantly, compare the same customer population. A business cannot take LTV from its best retained enterprise accounts and CAC from a cheap consumer campaign.

Use the ratio to make a decision

Build a simple table for each meaningful customer cohort: acquisition spend, customers won, months retained, revenue, gross profit and payback. Compare scenarios in which churn is higher or expansion lower than forecast.

If a channel has a promising LTV:CAC ratio but extremely slow payback, adjust its budget rather than assuming the ratio alone justifies more spending.
