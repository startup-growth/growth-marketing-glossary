Activation and retention

Customer Retention

Customer retention measures how many customers keep using or paying for a product after their first experience. A retained customer must do something that counts as continuing value. Simply leaving an account open is often too weak.

Read the rows, not just the dashboard total

Suppose a subscription app gains 500 customers in January. Of those original 500:

  • 250 use the product in week one.
  • 140 use it in week four.
  • 100 use it in week twelve.

Week-four retention is 140 ÷ 500 = 28%, using a week-four activity definition. The twelve-week rate is 20%. These percentages have the same original denominator.

Now suppose the business doubles acquisition in February. Total active users may rise, even if February customers retain less well than January customers. A company-wide usage graph can conceal that deterioration. A cohort table will show the difference.

Find out why customers stop

Look at behavior just before inactivity. In a scheduling app, one segment might quit because calendar imports fail. Another might keep using the service but stop paying after a temporary discount ends. Those require very different fixes.

Interview customers who left and customers who stayed. Segment by acquisition channel, plan, first-week behavior and customer type. Avoid assuming that an action correlated with retention automatically caused it.

Choose a sensible intervention

If customers never complete setup, work on activation. If users reach value but forget to return, a well-timed reminder could help. If they leave because the product is unreliable, another email sequence is unlikely to repair it.

For a weekly-use product, track weekly cohorts. For an annual tax-filing service, evaluating week-four activity could be absurd. Match the interval to the product.

Track churn rate alongside retained customers. Retention affects lifetime value and determines how much acquisition spend the business can recover.
