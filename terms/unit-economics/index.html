Economics and monetization

Unit Economics

Unit economics asks what a business earns and spends for one customer, order or transaction. It's a way to check whether more sales are likely to create more contribution or simply make the business lose money faster.

One delivery order, line by line

Consider a hypothetical ecommerce order:

  • Listed order price is $70, before the promotional discount.
  • Product cost is $28.
  • Fulfillment and delivery cost $8.
  • Payment and transaction fees cost $2.
  • A promotional discount costs $10.

After those costs, the order contributes $70 - $28 - $8 - $2 - $10 = $22, using the pre-discount listed price.

If paid acquisition costs $25 to get that customer, the first order is $3 contribution-negative after acquisition. That might be acceptable if the customer returns without another $25 of acquisition spend. It might be disastrous if almost nobody buys twice.

Use the right unit

An order-level view reveals whether transactions are profitable before acquisition. A customer-level view adds repeat purchases, returns and service costs over time. Subscription companies often need an account-level view because revenue arrives over months or years.

Decide whether refunds, chargebacks, support and discounts are included in the margin. Changing the definition midway through a report makes the comparison meaningless.

What action follows?

If orders are contribution-negative before advertising, raising bids is unlikely to save the business. Investigate fulfillment costs, pricing, discounting or product margin first.

If initial orders are profitable but acquisition costs too much, improve conversion, targeting or repeat purchasing. Use CAC and LTV to assess the whole relationship.

Unit economics is what turns a “successful” marketing campaign into a business decision: can we afford to get more customers like these?
