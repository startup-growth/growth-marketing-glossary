North Star Metric

A north star metric is one measure a product team uses to represent the value customers receive. It is most useful when the work of different teams can plausibly improve it, and when it connects to durable business results.

A booking product needs completed bookings

A scheduling platform might be tempted to choose “appointments created” as its north star. But canceled appointments and no-shows can inflate the count without helping patients or clinics.

A better candidate could be successfully completed appointments per month, provided the company can verify that completion reliably.

Suppose this month:

  • 400 clinics were active.
  • Each active clinic scheduled 100 appointments on average.
  • 85% of those appointments were completed.

The north star outcome would be 400 × 100 × 85% = 34,000 completed appointments.

That decomposition makes decisions practical. The marketing team can attract suitable clinics. Product can reduce friction in scheduling. Operations may be able to lower no-show rates. Each team moves an input that changes the customer outcome.

Check that the metric is hard to game

If someone can lift the metric by creating pointless appointments, the definition is weak. Track guardrails such as customer satisfaction, cancellations and whether clinics remain active.

A north star does not replace revenue, margin or cash measures. It tells teams what customer activity they believe leads toward a healthy business.

Try it in a planning meeting

Ask each team to name the input it influences and the specific work proposed to improve it. “Increase engagement” is too vague. “Help new clinics publish their first booking page within one day” is something a team can build and measure.

Review whether improvements in the north star are actually associated with retention and commercial outcomes. If they are not, change the model.
