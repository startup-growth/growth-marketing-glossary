Cohort Analysis

Cohort analysis compares customers who started at a similar time or share a meaningful characteristic. It is especially useful when overall growth hides deterioration in what happens to each new group of customers.

Build a simple retention table

Export all new paying customers with an acquisition month and a flag for whether they were active in each later month.

Imagine the resulting table shows:

  • January cohort: 200 customers; 160 active one month later, or 80%.
  • February cohort: 250 customers; 175 active one month later, or 70%.
  • March cohort: 300 customers; 180 active one month later, or 60%.

The business is acquiring more customers each month, but the newer groups are doing worse at the same age. Total active customers could still rise while early retention deteriorates.

Without cohorts, a dashboard might reward the acquisition team for growing the subscriber base while overlooking the quality problem.

Add context before diagnosing

Break the cohorts down by campaign, plan and customer type. If the March cohort came mainly from a large discount promotion, that may explain some of the decline. If all channels weakened after a product release, investigate onboarding or reliability.

Be precise about timing. “Month one” should mean the same elapsed period for every cohort, not the next calendar month for one and a six-week interval for another.

Find a next action

Suppose new customers who connect a data source within a week retain much better. Review whether the setup process changed and test interventions that help users complete it. The correlation is useful for investigation, but does not prove the connection event causes retention.

Activation can serve as an early signal; LTV benefits from tracking the revenue and gross profit of the same cohorts.

A useful cohort chart should lead to a decision: change targeting, fix onboarding, stop a promotion or learn why one segment is succeeding.
