Activation and retention

Lifecycle Marketing

Lifecycle marketing uses messages and product experiences to help people take the next useful step in their relationship with a company. It usually involves email, but in-app messages, push notifications and customer success outreach can all be part of it.

The best trigger is often what a customer has actually done, rather than the number of days since signup.

Three customers should not get the same email

A hypothetical analytics product has three new accounts:

  • Account A signed up but has not connected its data.
  • Account B connected its data but has not created a first report.
  • Account C created a report and invited two colleagues.

Sending all three the same “Finish setting up your account” email is a poor use of customer information.

Account A needs help with the connection. Account B needs a useful report template or a clear next step. Account C might benefit from guidance on sharing reports or scheduling them.

A basic lifecycle program can trigger on those events, while suppressing messages after the account completes the action. If the product already knows the customer succeeded, it should not send a reminder to do the thing again.

How to measure whether email helped

Suppose a trigger reaches 2,000 eligible accounts. Randomly withhold the email from a suitable portion, if testing is feasible and appropriate. Compare seven-day activation between the emailed and holdout groups rather than counting clicks and assuming the message caused all subsequent activations.

Watch unsubscribe rates, complaints and support volume. An email can prompt an immediate click while irritating customers enough to reduce longer-term retention.

The boundary with growth

Growth marketing covers the wider acquisition-to-retention system. Lifecycle roles usually specialize in the moments after a company can identify a person or account. They often own segmentation, campaign logic, experimentation and deliverability.

Start with one high-value broken journey. Build the corresponding trigger, measure the outcome, and then move to the next failure. A complicated thirty-email sequence built before understanding customers tends to automate the wrong assumptions.
