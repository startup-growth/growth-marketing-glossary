Growth Funnel

A growth funnel shows how many people move through the steps leading to a useful business outcome. It helps identify where interested visitors stop making progress.

Follow 10,000 visitors through a trial

A hypothetical analytics product reports these events in a month:

  • 10,000 eligible visitors view the signup page.
  • 600 start the signup form.
  • 450 finish creating an account.
  • 90 connect a data source and create a first report.
  • 35 become paying customers.

The signup-page-to-paid rate is 35 ÷ 10,000 = 0.35%. But the more striking drop occurs after signup: only 90 ÷ 450 = 20% of new accounts produce their first report.

A common mistake is to optimize the form because it is visible and easy to edit. That might increase account creations while leaving the real obstacle untouched. The analyst should inspect the 360 accounts that signed up but never reached a report.

Define events before trusting the chart

For this funnel, the team should be able to say precisely what each event means. Does “account created” fire after email verification or before it? Does “report created” count an empty report? Are returning customers excluded from the new-trial cohort? Do users get seven days or thirty days to convert?

Use a stable person or account identifier and a consistent time window. Otherwise, a page reload can count as an extra conversion and slow-moving customers can appear to have vanished.

Decide what to fix

If customers abandon setup because the API key instructions are confusing, the next work is a documentation or onboarding change. If most visitors start signup but cannot finish on mobile, fixing the form may be urgent. The funnel tells the team *where to investigate*, not why the problem exists.

Then compare new customers at the same age using cohort analysis. A controlled experiment can establish whether the proposed fix increased activation rather than just coinciding with a good month.
