Experiments and measurement

Growth Experimentation

Growth experimentation means testing a particular change against a measurable customer or business result. A good experiment answers a decision the team actually has to make, such as whether to keep a new onboarding flow or spend more on a channel.

A shorter form that produces worse customers

A hypothetical SaaS team shortens its trial signup form. It randomly splits 4,000 eligible visitors into two equal groups:

  • Current form: 420 registrations, 130 accounts that activate within seven days.
  • Short form: 475 registrations, 128 accounts that activate within seven days.

The short form has a higher registration rate: 23.75% versus 21%. But the meaningful downstream outcome is virtually unchanged and slightly lower in the treatment group.

A team that reports only form submissions could call this a win. A team that cares about acquired, activated customers will be much more cautious. Those raw differences also need uncertainty estimates; they do not establish a statistically meaningful harm.

Set up the test before launch

Write the hypothesis and primary metric before looking at results. In this case:

  • Hypothesis: removing optional fields will increase seven-day activated accounts per eligible visitor.
  • Unit of assignment: visitor, with stable assignment across return visits.
  • Primary metric: seven-day activation rate from all assigned eligible visitors.
  • Diagnostic metric: registration completion.
  • Guardrail: qualified account rate, plus any relevant spam or support volume.

Estimate the sample needed to detect a useful change. Run the experiment for an appropriate duration and preserve delayed conversions.

What happens when the result is inconclusive?

You can keep the current form, review qualitative feedback, or run a better-powered test if the decision justifies the expense. “Not statistically significant” does not mean “the forms are identical.”

A/B testing is one experimental method. Incrementality tests can answer related questions about advertising spend. The common discipline is making the comparison credible.
