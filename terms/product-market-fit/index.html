Product-Market Fit

Product-market fit is when a specific group of customers gets enough value from a product to keep choosing it. It shows up in behavior: repeat use, renewals, recommendations and customers who would have trouble replacing the product.

A burst of new signups, especially after heavy promotion, is weak evidence on its own.

Look at the customers who stay

Take a hypothetical scheduling product for small clinics. Eighty clinics begin a paid trial in January. Fifty-two pay for a first month. Three months later, forty-six of those original eighty are still paying.

A growth team should look beyond the headline conversion rate. Are the retained clinics actively managing appointments? Do they use the product across their staff? What makes the fourteen who stopped paying different?

Suppose clinics with two or more receptionists retain well, while solo practitioners repeatedly cancel because setup takes too much effort. The company may have good fit with multi-person practices and poor fit with solo users. Calling the whole product “in product-market fit” hides that difference.

Do the legwork

Review cancellation reasons, talk to recent customers, watch setup sessions, and compare retention by customer type and signup month. Look for repeatable evidence that a clear group gets continuing value.

A survey asking whether customers would be disappointed if the product disappeared can add useful context, but it should not replace payment and usage data.

What changes the acquisition plan?

If multi-person practices retain and expand, shift messaging and acquisition toward that group. Make the onboarding experience work for them. Do not buy more solo-practitioner traffic merely because it is cheap.

That choice affects strategy, CAC and LTV. A customer who signs up and cancels after thirty days may look good in an ad dashboard while making the business poorer.

Fit can change as prices, competition and customer needs change. Keep looking at new cohorts instead of relying on an old launch story.
