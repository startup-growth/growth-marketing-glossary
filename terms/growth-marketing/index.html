Growth Marketing

Growth marketing is the work of getting more of the right people to become successful customers. It includes finding customers, helping them get value from a product, and keeping them long enough for the business to work. Depending on the company, the job might involve paid ads, SEO, onboarding, analytics or all four.

The campaign that looked successful

Consider a hypothetical telehealth service running Google Ads:

  • Ad spend: $8,000.
  • Landing-page visits: 4,000.
  • Patients who start booking: 400.
  • Patients who complete a consultation: 80.
  • First-month gross profit from those consultations: $6,400.

A paid media report might celebrate 400 booking starts at $20 each. But the business paid $100 for each completed consultation and earned only $80 of gross profit per consultation that month. The missing information is between the landing page and the actual appointment.

The marketer opens the booking funnel, checks it on a phone, and discovers that the eligibility questionnaire frequently rejects people only after they have chosen an appointment time. That is a product and landing-page problem, not something a better bid strategy will fix.

What the next week of work looks like

The team could move eligibility questions earlier, explain geographic restrictions on the ad landing page, and measure completed consultations by campaign rather than form starts. The relevant owners might include a designer, an engineer, operations and whoever manages paid acquisition.

That is a typical growth problem: improving an outcome that crosses several teams. A performance marketer might own the ad account. A growth marketer with wider responsibility can also work on the broken conversion path.

Which numbers matter?

Visits and clicks are useful diagnostics. They are not the business outcome. In this example the team should monitor completed consultations, patient acquisition cost, cancellations and whether the economics improve over subsequent visits.

Funnel analysis locates the drop-off. Experimentation can test a proposed change. Retention shows whether acquired patients come back.
