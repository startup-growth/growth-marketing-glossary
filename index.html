Growth Marketing Glossary

33 growth marketing terms explained with plain-language definitions, practical context, and detailed guides.

Find out what customer acquisition cost includes, how to define activation, why retention changes the economics of growth, and when an experiment supports a decision. Each term below gives you the essentials and links to a longer example.

What is growth marketing?

Growth marketing is the work of getting more of the right people to become successful customers. It covers how someone discovers a product, reaches a useful result, becomes a paying customer, and continues to find value.

A campaign can produce inexpensive signups while failing to create customers. If few people finish onboarding or return, reducing the cost per signup will do little for the business. A growth team investigates what happens after signup, tests changes, and measures whether more customers succeed.

Growth marketing terms and definitions A to Z

33 terms

  • A/B Testing

    A/B testing randomly assigns eligible people to different versions of a page, message, or product experience. A team might test two signup flows and compare completed, qualified registrations. Random assignment helps distinguish the effect of the change from differences in the visitors who saw it.

  • AARRR Framework

    AARRR organizes growth measurement into Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, and Revenue. Acquisition brings people in; activation asks whether they received value; retention tracks who comes back. Referral and revenue describe how customers spread adoption and how the business earns money. Each stage needs an observable event.

  • Activation Rate

    Activation rate is the share of eligible new users who reach a meaningful first-value event within a defined period. If 240 of 800 signups complete that action within seven days, seven-day activation is 30%. Pick an event associated with later customer success, rather than an easy-to-count click.

  • CAC Payback Period

    CAC payback period estimates how long customer gross profit takes to recover the acquisition cost. At $300 CAC and $50 in monthly gross profit, simple payback is six months if that contribution remains constant. Longer payback ties up cash even when estimated lifetime value is high.

  • Churn Rate

    Churn rate measures the share of customers or recurring revenue lost over a period. If 20 of the 500 customers present at the beginning of a month cancel, customer churn is 4%. Revenue churn may tell a different story when the departing customers pay more or less than average.

  • Cohort Analysis

    Cohort analysis follows customers who share a start date or meaningful characteristic. Comparing January and February signups after the same number of weeks reveals whether newer customers retain better. Total active users can rise while each new cohort performs worse, so aggregate growth can hide a problem.

  • Conversion Rate

    Conversion rate is the share of eligible people who complete a defined action. If 60 out of 1,500 landing-page visitors book a call, visitor-to-call conversion is 4%. State the denominator, action, and time window. Starting a form and completing a qualified booking are different outcomes.

  • Conversion Rate Optimization (CRO)

    Conversion rate optimization (CRO) improves how many people complete a valuable action in a website or product flow. A team might explain eligibility earlier or remove a confusing checkout step. Completed purchases and qualified leads matter more than merely increasing form starts, especially when refunds rise.

  • Customer Acquisition

    Customer acquisition turns prospective buyers into new customers. The work includes choosing an audience, reaching it through relevant channels, and making it possible to buy or subscribe. Website visits and raw lead counts can help diagnose the process, but neither automatically represents a new customer.

  • Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)

    Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is the average expense of winning a new customer. Fully loaded CAC can include media, agency fees, and relevant sales and marketing labor. If $20,000 in acquisition expenses produces 100 paying customers, CAC is $200 under that definition. Long sales cycles require careful matching of costs and customers.

  • Customer Lifetime Value (CLV/LTV)

    Customer lifetime value (CLV or LTV) estimates the economic value generated throughout a customer relationship. Revenue-based and gross-profit-based estimates answer different questions. For acquisition decisions, gross-profit LTV is often more useful because retention, margins, and support costs affect how much value remains after delivery.

  • Customer Retention

    Customer retention measures whether existing customers keep using or buying a product over time. A subscription business might track renewals, while a collaboration app may measure repeat meaningful use. Compare cohorts at similar ages so new signups do not obscure what happens to customers after their first visit.

  • Demand Generation

    Demand generation builds familiarity, interest, and buying intent before people are ready to convert. Useful research, educational content, and events may contribute to it. Their effects can appear later in branded search or sales conversations, so last-click lead totals capture only part of the result.

  • Experiment Guardrail Metrics

    Experiment guardrail metrics monitor unintended harm from a change. A signup redesign may increase registrations while lowering qualified customers or creating extra support tickets. The primary measure shows whether the intended outcome improved; guardrails help decide whether the change should stay.

  • Funnel Analysis

    Funnel analysis measures how people move through a sequence and where they drop out. If 1,000 visitors view pricing, 300 start signup, and 120 finish, the step-level changes identify where to investigate. The team still needs to learn whether users were eligible and what caused them to leave.

  • Growth Experimentation

    Growth experimentation tests a specific hypothesis against a customer or business outcome. A useful test starts with the decision the team needs to make, then defines the outcome, comparison, and guardrails. A rise in button clicks is little help if the change reduces completed purchases.

  • Growth Funnel

    A growth funnel represents the steps people take from discovering a product to becoming successful customers. It shows where acquisition, signup, activation, and purchase lose people. Funnels are useful for finding bottlenecks; they do not show whether the output of one cycle creates future demand.

  • Growth Loops

    A growth loop is a repeating mechanism where an output of using or growing a product feeds another cycle of growth. A collaboration user might invite a colleague who becomes active and invites others. The loop depends on those invitations producing useful participation, rather than simply being sent.

  • Growth Marketing

    Growth marketing connects customer acquisition, activation, retention, and revenue to improve how a business grows. The work may include paid campaigns, onboarding, lifecycle messages, analytics, and product changes. The team follows results beyond the first click to see whether acquired customers receive lasting value.

  • Growth Strategy

    A growth strategy identifies the customer segment to prioritize, the value it needs, the channels that can reach it, and the resources required. It also spells out what will wait. A startup might focus on improving activation for one segment before increasing ad spend across several channels.

  • Incrementality Testing

    Incrementality testing estimates how many additional outcomes an intervention caused beyond what would have happened anyway. A holdout or geographic comparison can reveal that a retargeting campaign received credit for people who were already likely to buy. That evidence matters when making budget decisions.

  • Lifecycle Marketing

    Lifecycle marketing adapts communication to a customer's stage and behavior. A new signup may need help reaching first value, an active customer may need a relevant feature, and an at-risk customer may need support. The goal is progress in the relationship, rather than sending everyone the same sequence.

  • LTV:CAC Ratio

    The LTV:CAC ratio compares estimated lifetime customer value with the cost to acquire a customer. If gross-profit LTV is $600 and CAC is $200, the ratio is 3:1. The estimate depends on retention and costs, while the ratio alone cannot tell you how quickly the acquisition expense is recovered.

  • Marketing Attribution

    Marketing attribution assigns conversion credit to marketing touchpoints such as ads, search, referrals, and email. First-touch and last-touch models can report different winners for the same customer. Attribution describes observed paths to purchase, but credited conversions do not prove a channel created additional demand.

  • Marketing Mix Modeling (MMM)

    Marketing mix modeling (MMM) uses aggregated sales, spending, and contextual data to estimate different activities' contributions to business outcomes. It can include effects missing from click-based tracking, such as offline campaigns. The estimates depend on data quality, meaningful variation, and reasonable assumptions.

  • Net Revenue Retention (NRR)

    Net revenue retention (NRR) measures how recurring revenue from an existing customer group changes after expansion, downgrades, and cancellations. New-customer revenue is excluded. Starting with $100,000, adding $10,000 in expansion, and losing $8,000 produces 102% NRR. That figure can coexist with substantial customer churn.

  • North Star Metric

    A North Star metric captures a recurring outcome that represents customer value and helps align teams. A marketplace might track successful transactions rather than raw site visits. Supporting measures explain how acquisition, activation, and retention contribute, while the main metric keeps attention on value delivered.

  • Performance Marketing

    Performance marketing manages campaigns against measurable outcomes such as qualified leads, sales, and customer acquisition cost. It often concentrates on paid channels and the economics of spending. It overlaps with growth marketing, although a wider growth remit can include onboarding, retention, and changes to the product experience.

  • Product-Led Growth (PLG)

    Product-led growth (PLG) uses the product experience to help people discover, adopt, and expand usage. Free trials, self-service onboarding, shared work, and collaboration invitations can support it. The key question is whether people can experience the value directly and have a reason to continue using the product.

  • Product-Market Fit

    Product-market fit describes a product that meets a strong, recurring need in a specific market segment. Sustained use, renewals, referrals, and customers who would struggle to replace the product offer useful evidence. Rapid signup growth is weaker evidence when most people leave after trying it.

  • Search Engine Optimization (SEO)

    Search engine optimization (SEO) improves a site's ability to appear for relevant organic searches. It includes useful content, crawlable pages, clear structure, and earned credibility. For growth teams, the important outcome is qualified visitors who can become successful customers, not search traffic by itself.

  • Time to Value (TTV)

    Time to value (TTV) is the time between a customer's starting point and the first meaningful benefit from a product. Completing setup might happen before value arrives. Teams study where new users get stuck and compare different customer groups to find opportunities to shorten the path to a useful result.

  • Unit Economics

    Unit economics examines revenue and costs associated with serving a customer, order, or other business unit. It helps show whether acquiring more customers produces enough contribution after variable costs. CAC, lifetime value, and payback connect those unit-level results to the decision to scale spending.

How these growth metrics fit together

A hypothetical software company spends $6,000 on a paid campaign that brings 10,000 visitors to its website. The campaign's cost per visitor looks promising, but customer outcomes tell a more useful story.

  • 500 people sign up. The visitor-to-signup conversion rate is 500 divided by 10,000, or 5%.
  • 200 reach first value within seven days. The seven-day activation rate is 200 divided by 500, or 40%.
  • 50 become paying customers. The signup-to-paid conversion rate is 50 divided by 500, or 10%.
  • Advertising cost per paying customer is $120. That is $6,000 divided by 50. Fully loaded CAC also includes relevant acquisition costs beyond the ad spend.

The team now has a useful question: why did 300 signups fail to reach first value? They could inspect onboarding, interview affected users, and run an experiment on a proposed fix. They would monitor retention and CAC payback to learn whether better activation leads to sustainable growth.

These numbers are hypothetical examples, not industry benchmarks.

Common questions about growth marketing

How is growth marketing different from performance marketing?

Performance marketing typically focuses on measurable campaign results and the economics of spending. Growth marketing also examines what happens after acquisition, including activation, repeat use, monetization, and product changes. The two can overlap, particularly on small teams.

Which growth marketing metrics should a startup track first?

Start with the event that shows customers received value. Measure how many new customers reach it and whether they return. If the business pays to acquire customers, connect those measures to CAC and the economics of revenue or contribution. The appropriate events differ across subscription products, marketplaces, and transactional businesses.

What is the difference between a growth funnel and a growth loop?

A growth funnel follows people through a sequence, such as visit, signup, activation, and purchase. A growth loop explains how the output of one cycle feeds another. Active customers who share useful content may attract new visitors, starting another cycle.

How do attribution and incrementality differ?

Attribution describes which touchpoints receive credit for a conversion. Incrementality testing estimates how many extra outcomes marketing caused beyond what would have happened anyway. Both inform spending decisions, but they answer different questions.
